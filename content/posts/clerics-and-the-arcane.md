Written language was first transcribed by monks. Software development is at that same stage — the clerics hoard the sacred texts and arcane languages, shaping ideas and meaning into information flows they control.

The UX of terminal-driven development is hostile to someone who thinks at the architecture level. When written language was first set out, it was the monks doing the hand transcriptions. That is where software dev is at.

The disconnection: typing fingers don't connect with a picture in my head of what commands look like or how directories are structured. I excel with concepts — flow, relationships, *why* — but connecting fingers with the ideas of commands, syntax, and the dictionary of CLI just doesn't click.

That's the gap. When I called Hermes a dunce, the frustration came from that disconnection — from not being able to accomplish the "quick easy" thing I set out to do. Because of lack of planning, I think I can load a Pi up in 5 minutes and am very frustrated an hour later flipping between three sessions and typing RSA key characters char by char while hunting and pecking on a phone screen that scrolls, shuts off, or dims.

This has turned me off of GitHub and a lot of projects because I am not fluent in their arcane languages.

But I *do* want to type and learn. Hermes and the last two months have pushed my typing and reasoning. I have been storytelling for a few years now, not the cyber jockey I once was. And I am learning more through therapy about my ADHD.

The answer is not giving up on the terminal — it's having a bridge. Someone who sees the whole command tree and feeds one short, clear line at a time.

— Stuart, July 2026

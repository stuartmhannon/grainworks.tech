A single RA3 walkthrough at the Johnson residence, from Jeff's notes: twenty-one zones. Three floors worth of dimmers, keypads, fan controls, shades, and Lumaris tape runs. The spec runs nine feet on the page. Behind it is something harder to see: the geometry of where people actually stand in a room, and the allocation of a finite number of circuits.

That is the part field notes expose that proposals hide.

## The Shorthand Is the Map

Dimmmers marked by position — left of porch, right of bed, right of bath door — each location chosen by reach and sightline, not by convention. A keypad at the kitchen entry and another by the island sink because those are the two places someone walks into the room with their hands full. A dimmer repeat in the dining room because the primary load is at the other end of the counter. The 2g 1d 1r entries are a floor plan in code, and they read like constraints: how many gangs fit in this box, which loads are circuited together, and whether there is room in the stud bay for an extra wire.

The 4-gang bathroom — two dimmers, one non-switch fan, one switch — is a small room that serves four distinct functions controlled from one point. That is the kind of density that forces tradeoffs. One more function means a larger box or a dual-load module somewhere else.

## Ambiguities That Walkthroughs Catch

A note on the master bedroom right of bath: "1g 1hykp (or 3g)." That parenthetical means the first plan did not fit. A hybrid keypad plus a dimmer is at least two devices in one gang; the actual wall box might need to become three. The walkthrough surfaces this early, which is the whole point of the walkthrough.

Lumaris under the kitchen island — 20 feet through slim diffusion under a countertop overhang — cannot be measured from a plan. The millwork condition, the available power feed, and the clearance from the undermount sink are real-world constraints that only exist in the room. The bar Lumaris note says it plainly: "Would need new jumper wires run — not sure if possible with millwork condition." That is an honest walkthrough. The answer is not known yet, and the note says so.

Shade spec is a one-liner: "ADD SHADE CHECK SPEC." It is a reminder, not an oversight. The walkthrough owner is tracking it.

## What the Walking Produces

The Johnson walkthrough produced three things that a proposal alone never would:

1. **Positional judgment calls.** Where the keypad goes relative to the door swing, the counter edge, and the sightline to the main fixture. These are ergonomic decisions made in three dimensions, not from an elevation.

2. **System architecture revealed by adjacency.** A dual dimmer in the same gang as a keypad is not just a device count. It is a decision about whether those two loads need independent dimming or whether a keypad scene can handle them together. The field note encodes this in the entry itself.

3. **A running gap list.** The Lumaris jumper uncertainty. The shade spec that needs filling. The 1g 1hykp that might need a 3-gang rework. Real projects have real gaps. The walkthrough surfaces them while they are cheap to fix.

## The Note Itself

The last thing worth noticing is the field note itself. It is not clean. It does not need to be. It is a working document written from a walkthrough with a contractor standing next to you, and it captures the signal before it decays. Every professional trades the polish of a final document for the completeness of a first pass. The rule is: capture first, organize later.

This field note, raw and unglossed, holds more information about the Johnson residence than any polished proposal could. Because the proposal would have resolved the ambiguities. The field note keeps them visible, which is, in the end, the point of the exercise.
